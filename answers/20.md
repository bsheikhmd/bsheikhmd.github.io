*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

For everyday life, mostly yes. For demanding sports, often not.

A knee replacement reliably eases pain and makes walking, stairs and getting up from a chair easier. Most of the gain comes in the first three months.

Easier is not the same as normal. Six months to a year after surgery, people with a replacement are still slower getting up from a chair and climbing stairs than people the same age who never had knee trouble, and their thigh muscles stay weaker. In one study, more than half still used the handrail on stairs two years after surgery, about as many as before surgery. Some still take stairs one step at a time, bringing both feet onto each step. The best stair results went to younger, stronger people who needed no handrail before surgery.

Kneeling, squatting, turning quickly and carrying loads also remain harder. Swimming, golf and stationary cycling come out about the same.

Most people return to some sport, mostly low-impact. High-impact sports usually drop off, often out of caution rather than because of the knee itself. That evidence is weak.

Many patients expect a normal knee. Unmet expectations are a leading reason for disappointment, even after a good result.

In general:

- Expect less pain and easier daily life, not the knee of a younger person.
- Low-impact activity is the usual way back to sport.
- Tell your surgeon before surgery what you hope to do afterward.

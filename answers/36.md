*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

It is fairly common. Often it is because straightening the leg changes how the ankle carries weight, and it often eases, though not always.

A knee that has slowly become bowed or knock-kneed changes how the ankle and heel sit under it. Over years, the heel and ankle tilt to keep the foot flat on the ground. A knee replacement straightens the leg in one operation, and the ankle and heel then have to adjust again.

In one study, about 1 in 4 people reported new or worse ankle symptoms three months after surgery. In other studies, ankle pain became less common after surgery on average, especially when the heel had been turned outward beforehand. These studies are small, and the overall evidence is low quality.

Ankle pain afterward is more likely in people who already had ankle arthritis, a heel turned inward, or an ankle tilted before surgery, and after very large corrections of a bowed or knock-kneed leg. Studies disagree on whether a stiff heel matters. Higher body weight was linked in one study. In people over 65, the heel adjusted less to the new alignment.

How long it lasts is not well studied. In one study, ankle pain became less common over the first year, and an ankle tilted before surgery predicted pain still present at a year. After very large corrections, symptoms were still present about five years later.

Straightening the leg usually also makes it a little longer. No study has linked that to ankle pain. Whether a high-arched or flat foot before surgery makes ankle pain more likely has not been studied.

In general:

- Tell your surgeon before surgery about any ankle pain, ankle arthritis or foot problem.
- New ankle pain after a knee replacement is worth mentioning at follow-up, even when the knee is doing well.
- Pain, swelling or tenderness in the calf needs to be seen promptly.

*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Longer than most people expect. Daily activities usually return within weeks, but the knee keeps improving for about a year.

According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, most people resume most normal daily activities within 3 to 6 weeks. They need help with cooking, shopping, bathing and laundry for several weeks, and some pain with activity and at night is common for several weeks. No study has reported when people typically stop using a walker or a cane.

The largest gains come in the first months. In a Canadian study, knee scores improved from 6 weeks to 6 months, and again from 6 months to 1 year, but not from 1 to 2 years. In a US study of 4,342 knee replacements, most patients reached an acceptable level of symptoms by 4 months. Scores kept improving slightly for up to 5 years, and morning stiffness was among the slowest things to get better.

Recovery varies from person to person. In a British study, 8% of people had no improvement in pain and 21% no improvement in function at 1 year. Of those whose symptoms had not changed by 1 year, a third improved further in the second year.

The thigh muscles often stay weaker. In a Japanese study, fewer than half of knees reached thigh strength in the range of people the same age without knee arthritis 1 year after surgery. Those who did were more satisfied and did better with daily activities.

In the long run, the results hold. In a Korean study of 1,264 people, scores stayed better than before surgery for 15 years, though physical function declined slightly over time.

All of these studies are observational. They show a link, not proof of cause.

In general:

- Arrange help at home for the first several weeks.
- Judge progress over months, not days.
- Ask your therapist which thigh-strengthening exercises to keep doing after therapy ends.
- If pain gets worse or progress stops, tell your surgeon.

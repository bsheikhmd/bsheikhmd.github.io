*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Not clearly. Each approach has its own trade-offs, and neither has been shown to be better overall.

An anterior hip replacement reaches the hip through the front. A posterior one reaches it through the back. The new joint is the same either way.

Surgery through the front is often done on a special table that holds the leg by a boot at the foot, with live X-rays taken during the operation. Surgery through the back is usually done with the patient lying on their side on a standard table.

Trials comparing the two found less pain and a faster start in the first weeks when the hip was replaced through the front. Walking distance and hip muscle strength were slightly better in the first months. By a year, pain and function come out about the same.

Dislocation, the ball slipping out of the socket, has been less common after surgery through the front in several large studies, though pooled trials found no clear difference. People who have had a fusion in the lower back are more likely to dislocate, and in that group, dislocations were fewer with surgery through the front.

Other complications differ. Surgery through the front more often leaves a numb patch on the outer thigh and more often causes minor wound problems. Weakness from nerve injury is rare with either approach, but it tends to affect different nerves. Surgery through the front more often affects the nerve that straightens the knee. Surgery through the back more often affects the nerve that lifts the foot. Only about half of those injuries recover fully. Ankle injuries from the leg-holding table have been described but are rare.

Thigh bone fractures happen at about the same rate with either approach, but after surgery through the front they tend to appear in the first weeks, often without a fall. Extra bone forming around the new hip was less common after surgery through the front in one large study, though the severe kind was not.

The socket is more often placed in the target position through the front. Whether leg lengths come out more even is unclear.

For durability, overall repeat surgery rates look similar. Some registries found more loosening of the thigh-bone part after surgery through the front. Comparisons beyond about ten years do not exist yet.

Surgeons new to the front approach have more complications, which fall as they gain experience.

According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, there is no preferred approach, and each has specific risks and benefits.

In general:

- Ask your surgeon which approach they use most often, and how often they do it.
- Mention any lower back fusion or stiff lower back before surgery.
- New weakness in the thigh, trouble lifting the leg, or a foot that drags needs to be seen promptly.
- New thigh or groin pain in the first weeks, even without a fall, is worth reporting.
- Drainage, redness or a wound that reopens needs to be seen promptly.

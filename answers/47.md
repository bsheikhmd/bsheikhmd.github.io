*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Usually about the same in the end. Some people find the first days harder the second time, and the improvement can feel smaller.

When both knees are replaced in separate operations, most people end up with two knees that work about equally well. Many studies found the second knee improved less, but largely because it was less painful to begin with. Satisfaction was about the same for both.

The hospital stay is often slightly shorter the second time, and people more often go straight home. Several small studies found more pain after the second knee, especially when the operations were close together. A larger study found no difference. One large study found more emergency visits after the second operation, mostly for infection, with no difference in redo operations.

How the first knee went is the best guide to the second. A complication after the first knee made one after the second more likely. Still, in one study about three in four people who were unhappy with their first knee were satisfied with their second.

About one in five people planning a second knee do not go ahead within two years. The most common reasons were a slow recovery or a poor experience with the first knee, though some found the other knee had improved enough. Those who stopped had done less well with the knee they had.

At least six weeks between operations is linked to fewer complications. Beyond that, the best gap is not known. One study found women improved less with the second knee than with the first.

In general:

- If both knees need replacing, ask your surgeon whether one operation or two suits you.
- Tell your surgeon how the first recovery went, including anything that worried you.
- Ask how pain will be managed the second time.
- If the other knee has improved since the first operation, say so before scheduling.

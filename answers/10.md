*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Probably not as strictly as you were told. Hip precautions are the rules many people are given after a hip replacement: no bending past 90 degrees, no crossing the legs, no turning the foot inward. They were meant to keep the new hip from dislocating.

Those rules date from a time when dislocation was much more common. Dislocation has become steadily less common over the decades, and larger ball sizes in the new joint are a big part of the reason. So the precautions have been put to the test.

In randomized trials of first-time hip replacements, patients without precautions did not dislocate more often than patients with them, and they got back to daily life a little faster. That includes the posterior approach, the operation precautions were designed for.

The evidence is not airtight. Dislocation is uncommon, so even large studies have trouble detecting a small difference. Two large studies of hospitals that dropped precautions found slightly more dislocations afterward, though not enough to be sure the difference was real. Reviews rate the overall evidence as low certainty.

These studies were done in routine first-time replacements. They say little about a redo operation or a hip the surgeon considers higher risk.

In general:

- Follow your own surgeon's instructions. They reflect how your hip was put in.
- If the precautions are making daily life hard, ask whether you still need them.
- Where precautions are used, they usually last about six weeks, not for life.
- A redo operation is a different question from a first replacement.

*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Most teenagers do well.

The usual surgery straightens the curve and joins the bones of the curved part of the spine so they heal into one solid bone (spinal fusion). It is usually done through a cut down the middle of the back, or less often through the side of the body. The scar is where the cut was made. Metal rods and screws hold the spine straight and are usually left in. Some surgeons use smaller cuts.

For a child who still has a lot of growing left, another option is a surgery that uses a flexible cord instead of a fusion (tethering). The spine keeps its movement, but a second operation is needed much more often than after fusion.

Serious problems are uncommon. About 1 in 40 teenagers has one around the time of surgery, most often a problem with the wound. Damage to the nerves or spinal cord happens in fewer than 1 in 100.

Most teenagers go home after a few days, and some go home the day after surgery. On average, teenagers are back at school full days about seven weeks after surgery. Most return to sports within about a year. Surgeons usually allow contact sports later than running.

Surgery makes the rib hump smaller and the shoulders more level. Years later, back pain is less than in scoliosis that was not treated. The fused part of the back no longer bends.

In general:

- Ask your child's surgeon which surgery fits your child's curve and age.
- Plan for a few days in the hospital and several weeks away from school.
- Ask the surgeon when your teenager can start each sport.

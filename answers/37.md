*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Either is a reasonable choice for most people. Large record studies lean toward spinal, but trials have not shown a clear winner.

With general anesthesia, you are fully asleep. With a spinal, medicine injected in the lower back numbs you from the waist down.

Studies of hundreds of thousands of knee replacements from hospital records found fewer complications with spinal, including less pneumonia, fewer readmissions, and in some studies fewer deaths. One large study found less confusion after surgery with spinal. But record studies cannot fully account for why one person was given a spinal and another was not, and not every large study found a difference in deaths.

Trials that assigned people at random are few and small. They found similar pain, complications and hospital stays. One found people were up and walking sooner after a general anesthetic given entirely through the vein. Across studies, nausea was about the same with either. Another trial found knees worked slightly better a year after spinal. None was large enough to show whether one is safer.

A spinal has its own drawbacks. Trouble passing urine afterward is more common, and occasionally a spinal does not work well enough and general anesthesia is used instead.

An international group of anesthesia experts prefers spinal for knee replacement, but calls it a weak recommendation based on low-quality evidence.

In general:

- The choice is made with your anesthesiologist, based on your health.
- If being awake during surgery worries you, ask about sedation.
- Tell the anesthesia team about blood thinners, sleep apnea, lung disease or any past spine surgery.
- Trouble passing urine after surgery is worth reporting.

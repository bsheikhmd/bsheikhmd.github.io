*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Usually not in the last three months before surgery. The concern is infection of the new joint, one of the most serious problems after a knee replacement.

Several large studies, pooled together, found that a cortisone shot into the same knee within about three months of the replacement was linked to a higher rate of infection afterward. Shots given earlier were not clearly linked to more infection. Most hip and knee surgeons who were surveyed wait at least three months between a shot and surgery.

The evidence has limits. It comes from records, not trials, so it shows a link rather than proof of cause. Some studies, including a large national study from England, found no increase in problems after surgery.

Shots elsewhere, such as in the other knee or a shoulder, have not been studied the same way. In people with diabetes, a cortisone shot into a joint can raise blood sugar for a few days.

In general:

- Tell your surgeon about any cortisone shot in the months before surgery, in any joint.
- If surgery is already scheduled, ask before getting another shot in that knee.
- Before a cortisone shot in another joint, such as a shoulder or the other knee, talk it over with your surgeon first.
- People with diabetes may see higher sugar readings for a few days after a shot.

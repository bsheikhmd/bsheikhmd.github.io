*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Either way can work well, and the choice is mostly your surgeon's.

In a knee replacement, the underside of the kneecap can be covered with a plastic button, called resurfacing, or left as it is. In the largest trial, with more than 1,700 people, results five years later were the same either way. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, there is no difference between the two.

When many trials are pooled, some differences appear. Resurfacing led to somewhat less pain at the front of the knee and fewer repeat operations, while overall function came out about the same. The advantage shows up mainly with older implant designs. With modern implants shaped to suit an unresurfaced kneecap, it largely disappears.

National joint registries lean the same way. In two large registries, knees without resurfacing were redone somewhat more often for more than half of the implants studied, with no difference for most of the rest.

Kneecap fracture after resurfacing is rare, and no more common than without it.

If pain at the front of the knee continues, the kneecap can be resurfaced later, but this helps only about half of people.

Practice differs widely between countries. Most knees in the United States are resurfaced, while few are in Norway or Sweden. Evidence for specific groups, such as people with rheumatoid arthritis, is limited.

In general:

- Ask your surgeon whether they usually resurface the kneecap, and why.
- Ask which implant they plan to use.
- Pain at the front of the knee that does not settle is worth reporting.

*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

It can be, for the right person. Doing both knees in one operation carries more risk in the first weeks, mostly involving the heart, lungs and blood vessels.

Pooled studies find more blood clots in the lungs, more nervous-system complications such as stroke, and a higher death rate in the first months when both knees are done together. On the other hand, infection is less common, the hospital stay is shorter, and the knees work about as well in the end.

Severe deformity in both legs is a special case. Straightening a badly bowed or bent knee tends to lengthen that leg. In one small study, some people who had one knee done noticed a difference in leg length, while none of those who had both done did. Whether that should decide the timing is untested.

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons says both knees can be done together in carefully chosen patients, with caution, but not in people at high risk of heart or lung complications. Most of this evidence comes from hospital records rather than trials.

In general:

- General health, heart and lungs weigh more in this choice than the knees do.
- With severe deformity in both legs, ask how the timing affects leg length.
- If done separately, spacing the knees at least three months apart is linked to fewer complications.

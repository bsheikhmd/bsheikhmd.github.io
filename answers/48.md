*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

For most people, probably not much. Both work well, and the knee usually feels and works about the same either way.

In a cemented knee replacement, the parts are fixed to the bone with a fast-setting bone cement. In a cementless one, the parts are pressed into the bone, and bone grows into their rough surface over the following weeks. Some surgeons use a mix, cementing one part and not the other. Cement is used far more often and has the longest track record, but cementless knees are becoming more common in the United States.

According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, cemented, cementless and mixed fixation give similar function, complications and redo operations, though the evidence is conflicting. Randomized trials found no clear difference in how long the knee lasts or how well it works. Cementless operations were a little shorter, and in some studies bled more.

Large national joint registries, which follow hundreds of thousands of knees, found slightly more redo operations with cementless knees in several countries. The difference was small. In England, about 1 in 200 more knees needed a redo within ten years. Other registries found no difference. Cementless knees tend to go to younger people and to men, who have more redo operations anyway, which makes the comparison harder.

The particular implant matters. Some cementless designs did as well as their cemented versions, and others did worse.

Studies disagree on age. One pooled review found cementless knees lasted better in people under 60, while several registries found the opposite. In one American registry, women over 70 had more early redo operations with cementless knees.

In general:

- Ask your surgeon which kind of fixation they plan to use, and why.
- Ask how long they have used that particular implant and how it has done.

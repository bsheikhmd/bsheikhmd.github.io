*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Often spinal, when it suits your health. The evidence leans toward spinal, though most of it comes from hospital records rather than trials.

With general anesthesia, you are fully asleep. With a spinal, medicine injected in the lower back numbs you from the waist down.

Large record studies of hip replacements found fewer complications with spinal, including fewer breathing problems, heart problems and blood transfusions. Pooled studies found fewer deaths with spinal. But record studies cannot fully account for why one person was given a spinal and another was not.

Trials that assigned people at random are few and small. Pooled together, they found less nausea and a slightly shorter hospital stay with spinal. None was large enough to show whether one is safer.

For same-day hip replacement, no guideline recommends one type over the other. In a large study of same-day hip and knee replacements, serious complications were about equally rare with either, but people given a spinal had less pain and nausea and were more likely to go home that day. In one trial, a shorter-acting spinal got more people walking and home the same day.

A spinal has its own drawbacks. Trouble passing urine afterward is more common, and occasionally a spinal does not work well enough and general anesthesia is used instead.

An international group of anesthesia experts strongly recommends spinal for hip replacement, based on moderate- to low-quality evidence.

In general:

- The choice is made with your anesthesiologist, based on your health.
- If being awake during surgery worries you, ask about sedation.
- Tell the anesthesia team about blood thinners, sleep apnea, lung disease or any past spine surgery.
- Trouble passing urine after surgery is worth reporting.

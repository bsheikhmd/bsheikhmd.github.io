*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Some people do still have pain a year or more after a hip replacement. Usually it is mild, and most people are pleased with the result even so.

In large studies, a little over one in ten people had moderate or severe hip pain a year after surgery, yet about nine in ten were satisfied. After that, pain tends to stay where it is. In a national registry study that followed hip replacements for five years, about seven in ten people had a large improvement that lasted. The rest also kept their improvement, but at a lower level. How the hip was doing at six months predicted how it would be doing at five years. Severe pain several years out was uncommon, and less common than after a knee replacement.

Pain near a hip replacement does not always come from the implant. In a study of about 200 painful hip replacements sent for evaluation, the most common causes were irritation of the tissues around the hip, mostly a bursa on the side of the hip, and pain coming from the lower back. Wear of the plastic liner and loosening came next. Reactions to metal and infection were less common. According to a joint statement from the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons and hip surgeon groups, loosening and infection should be ruled out first.

Lasting pain is more likely in people with more pain before surgery, pain in other parts of the body, burning or tingling pain before surgery, depression or anxiety, a tendency to dwell on pain, or higher body weight, and possibly in women. These are links, not proof of cause.

In general:

- Pain on the side of the hip, or pain that seems to come from the back, is worth describing that way to your surgeon.
- Burning or tingling pain is worth describing that way too.
- Pain that returns or gets worse after the hip had been doing well is worth reporting.
- Pain with fever, swelling, redness or drainage from the scar needs to be seen promptly.

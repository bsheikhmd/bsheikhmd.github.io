*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

For most people having a routine knee replacement, it appears to be, though the studies do not all agree. For people at higher risk of clots, the evidence is thin.

After a knee replacement, blood clots can form in the veins of the leg and sometimes travel to the lungs. Nearly everyone is given something to lower that risk. Aspirin is one option. Prescription blood thinners, taken as pills or injections, are the others.

Several pooled reviews of trials found that aspirin prevented clots about as well as prescription blood thinners, with no clear difference in bleeding, wound problems or death. A large Canadian trial published in 2026 found that aspirin alone from the start worked as well as a short course of a blood-thinning pill followed by aspirin, with about the same bleeding.

A large Australian trial points the other way. Hospitals using aspirin had more clots than hospitals using a blood-thinning injection, mostly smaller clots below the knee. Deaths and major bleeding were no different.

The large trials enrolled mostly people at ordinary risk. Two large US record studies of people who had had a clot before found no more clots with aspirin than with prescription blood thinners. But surgeons chose who received aspirin, so those patients may have been at lower risk to begin with. Some reviews advise against aspirin alone for people at higher risk. Studies disagree on whether severe obesity on its own raises the risk of serious clots. No study found has tested aspirin after a knee replacement specifically in people with active cancer or an inherited clotting disorder.

Guidelines differ. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons guideline, from 2011, recommends a medicine or a compression device but found the evidence too unclear to name the best medicine. The American Society of Hematology says either aspirin or a blood thinner is reasonable, but rates the evidence as very low certainty. Neither makes a separate recommendation for people at higher risk. British guidance lists aspirin as one option.

In general:

- Which medicine to use is decided with your surgeon, based on your own risk of clots and of bleeding.
- Tell your surgeon about any past blood clot, cancer, clotting disorder or bleeding problem.
- Pain, swelling or tenderness in the calf needs to be seen promptly.
- Sudden shortness of breath or chest pain needs emergency care.

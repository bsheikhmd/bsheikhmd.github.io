*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Usually not in the last three months before surgery. The concern is infection of the new hip, one of the most serious problems after a hip replacement.

Large studies of hip replacements found that a cortisone shot into the same hip within about three months of surgery was linked to more infection afterward. Shots given earlier were not clearly linked. One study found that each extra shot in those last months added to the risk, and another linked more than two shots in the two years before surgery to more infection. When hips and knees were compared, the link was clearer for hips.

The evidence has limits. It comes from records, not trials, so it shows a link rather than proof of cause. Some smaller studies found no increase.

There is a second, separate concern. In a small share of people, the hip joint breaks down unusually fast in the months after a cortisone shot, more often when the arthritis is already advanced. Studies disagree on how often, and whether the shot is the cause is not settled.

According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, a cortisone shot can ease hip pain for a short time. But once a hip is bad enough to replace, a guideline from rheumatology and joint replacement surgeon groups advises against putting off surgery to try more shots. Its evidence is low quality.

In general:

- Tell your surgeon about any cortisone shot in the months before surgery, in any joint.
- If surgery is already scheduled, ask before getting another shot in that hip.
- Hip pain that gets much worse in the months after a shot is worth reporting.
- People with diabetes may see higher sugar readings after a shot.

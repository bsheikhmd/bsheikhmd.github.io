*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Yes. People with diabetes whose blood sugar is poorly controlled have more infections after knee replacement. But there is no agreed A1c number above which surgery becomes unsafe.

For most people, diabetes raises the risk only slightly. In the Danish national registry, redo operations for infection within five years occurred in about 17 of every 1,000 knee replacements in people with diabetes, and about 14 of every 1,000 in people without it. The risk varied widely with age, weight and blood vessel disease. In a Utah study of hip and knee replacements, joint infection was more common with type 1 diabetes than with type 2: about 7 in 100 compared with 4 in 100.

A1c measures average blood sugar over about three months. Studies looking for a cut-off have found numbers from about 7.7% to 9.7%, and a large 2025 study found that A1c predicts infection poorly. Shorter-term blood tests may do better. In a study of 1,119 knee replacements, patients with a fructosamine level above 293 were about 11 times more likely to develop joint infection within 12 weeks. A1c cut-offs of 7% and 7.5% showed no link.

Guidelines differ. The American Diabetes Association recommends an A1c under 8% within three months before planned surgery, and blood sugar between 100 and 180 mg/dL around the time of surgery. It also says surgery should not be postponed on the A1c number alone. The American College of Rheumatology and the American Association of Hip and Knee Surgeons suggest delaying surgery to improve blood sugar control, without naming a number, and rate the evidence as low or very low. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, surgeons should try to keep blood sugar under 126 mg/dL around and after knee replacement.

No trial has tested whether delaying surgery to lower A1c reduces infection. All of these studies are observational. They show a link, not proof that blood sugar itself is the cause.

In general:

- Ask your surgeon which blood sugar test and number they require before surgery.
- If your surgery is postponed, ask what number you need to reach.
- Work with the doctor who treats your diabetes to lower your blood sugar before surgery.
- Ask whether a fructosamine test would help in your case.

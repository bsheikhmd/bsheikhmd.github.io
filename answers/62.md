*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Usually it fades. A limp is common in the first months after a hip replacement, and for most people it gets much better.

The main reason is the muscles on the side of the hip, which keep the pelvis level with each step. Arthritis often weakens them before surgery, and surgery can disturb them further. How they work often stays below normal for months, sometimes a year or longer. Studies disagree widely on how many people still limp after a year. The surgical approach made little difference to limping two years or more after surgery.

A lasting limp matters. In a large registry study, people who still limped were less satisfied with their hip.

Less often, a limp has a specific cause: a difference in leg length, a torn tendon on the side of the hip, an injured nerve, or, rarely, a loose or infected implant.

Studies have not clearly shown that exercise makes a limp go away sooner. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, supervised therapy and exercising at home are both reasonable choices after a hip replacement.

In general:

- A limp that keeps improving is part of recovery.
- A limp that gets worse instead of better is worth reporting to your surgeon.
- A new limp with pain on the side of the hip, a foot that drags, or a hip that feels unstable needs to be seen.
- A limp with fever or pain at rest needs to be seen promptly.

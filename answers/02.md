*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

You can do it at home. The studies are fairly clear on that. When patients doing a home program are compared with patients going to a therapist, most outcomes come out the same, and several reviews have said routine formal therapy after a knee replacement may not be necessary.

That is not the whole picture. Those studies enroll people who agreed to be in a study and who did the program. They do not tell you much about the person who stops when it hurts, or who is not sure whether what they are feeling is normal.

That gap matters. The first weeks after a knee replacement are uncomfortable, and most people underdo it rather than overdo it. What the studies do show is that the people who fall behind early are the ones who need the help: trouble bending the knee in the first couple of weeks, trouble getting it fully straight, or struggling to manage at home. A therapist is not doing something to your knee that you cannot do yourself. A therapist is someone watching, pushing a little harder than you would push alone, and telling you when the pain you are feeling is the ordinary kind.

If you are the kind of person who will do it anyway, at home is fine. Most people are not sure which kind they are until they are already behind.

In general:

- Do it at home if you know you will actually do it.
- Go to a therapist if you think you will talk yourself out of it.
- Being uncomfortable in the first weeks is expected, not a sign to stop.
- If you are falling behind on bending the knee, say so early.

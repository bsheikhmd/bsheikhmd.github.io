*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Not necessarily. No study has shown that having a knee replaced early gives a better result, or that waiting makes the final result worse.

No trial has compared operating early with waiting. The evidence comes from studies that followed people before and after surgery, and it pulls in two directions. People who go into surgery with less pain and better function tend to end up a little better afterward, though they gain less from the operation. But people whose arthritis looks less advanced on the X-ray tend to end up with more pain and worse function. They are also less likely to reach a level they find acceptable a year later. These are links, not proof that timing caused the difference.

What waiting clearly does is cost time. While people wait for surgery, their pain, function, sleep, mood and quality of life tend to get worse. Pooled studies did not find that this lowered the result after surgery.

A guideline from rheumatologists and hip and knee surgeons says that once other treatments have stopped working for moderate to severe arthritis, surgery should not be put off to try more of them. It does support waiting to stop smoking or to get diabetes under better control. The evidence behind it is rated low.

In general:

- A knee replacement is for a knee that is limiting daily life now, not one that might later.
- When arthritis looks mild on the X-ray, a knee replacement tends to help less.
- If other treatments have stopped working, delaying surgery to try more of them has not been shown to help.
- Stopping smoking or improving blood sugar control before surgery is worth discussing with your physician.

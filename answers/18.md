*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

For most people it gets much better, but it does not always go away completely.

Pain drops sharply in the first three months after surgery, then keeps easing more slowly through the first year, and the improvement holds for ten years. Still, on average, people report some pain even at one year. In one study, most patients expected to be free of pain, but two years after surgery fewer than half were.

A smaller group is left with lasting, bothersome pain. Studies disagree on how many. For them, the pain still present at six months to a year tends to stay about the same. Often it has a nerve-type quality: burning, tingling or electric.

Lasting pain is more common in people with more knee pain before surgery, pain in other parts of the body, depression or poor mental health, a tendency to dwell on and fear pain, or severe pain in the first days after surgery. It is also more common in younger patients and in women. These are links from observational studies, not proof of cause.

Memory can mislead too. A year after surgery, many people remember inaccurately how bad the knee was beforehand, which can make the result seem better or worse than it is.

In general:

- Expect the knee to feel much better, not necessarily perfect.
- Burning or tingling pain is worth describing that way to your surgeon.
- Low mood or strong worry about pain before surgery is worth raising with your physician.
- Pain that is getting worse, or comes with swelling, warmth, redness or fever, needs to be seen promptly.

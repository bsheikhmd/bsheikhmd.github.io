*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Some people do, but most do not. Only a small share of people run after a hip replacement. Many who ran before surgery stop.

In one large survey, about half of the people who ran before surgery were no longer running five or more years later. The most common reasons were their surgeon's advice, fear of damaging the new hip, and pain. In another study, people who jogged before surgery were more likely to jog after it.

The worry is that pounding may wear the joint surfaces and loosen the implant. So far, studies have not shown this. In one study, joggers' X-rays showed no loosening or unusual wear after about five years. In another, runners did not need their hip redone significantly more often than non-runners. A Norwegian registry study looked at hips with a modern wear-resistant liner. More active people were not more likely to need their hip redone for loosening over about nine years. That study measured overall activity, not running.

The limits are real. The running studies are small and stop at about five years. People who chose to run were likely fitter to begin with. A hip replacement is expected to last far longer than five years. The younger you are, the more of your hip's life the evidence does not cover.

According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, most surgeons advise against running. Little research has measured fractures or dislocations during running.

In general:

- Tell your surgeon before surgery if you want to run afterward.
- Ask when you could start and how much. Advice varies among surgeons.
- If you choose not to run, walking, cycling and swimming are lower-impact options.

*Last reviewed: September 29, 2026*

Many people can, though not everyone, and not in the first weeks.

Squatting often gets easier. In one study, about twice as many people could squat easily after a hip replacement as before it. Those who never squatted mostly avoided it for fear of dislocating the hip. When hips were imaged during a squat, the implant parts did not touch. Sitting on the floor is harder. In a study of people whose hip was replaced through the back of the hip, about one in five could sit on the floor six months later, most often those who could do it before surgery.

Timing matters. In a large Danish study, dislocations in the first month mostly happened while lying or sitting, and those in the second and third months mostly while squatting or bending. No study has followed people doing these positions for years.

According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, a raised toilet seat and a high chair help avoid deep bending early on, and the legs should not be crossed for at least six to eight weeks. Studies disagree on whether such rules matter. Where raised seats and other precautions were dropped, dislocations did not increase, and a review rated the evidence for such equipment as very low quality. But the Danish study found fewer dislocations in hospitals that kept activity restrictions.

A fused lower back makes dislocation more likely, and so does a redo operation.

In general:

- Ask your surgeon when deep positions are allowed for your hip, and whether your implant or approach changes that.
- A fused lower back or a redo operation is a reason to ask before trying deep positions.
- A hip that suddenly gives way with severe pain needs to be seen right away.
